From NCR to AI: how I check agent output.

On a construction site, nothing is finished because someone says it is. It is finished when the cause of every defect has been found, fixed and checked. I build AI workflows the same way, and this is why.

What an NCR is

From 2021 to 2024 I worked as a site engineer on structural QA/QC at the Tun Razak Exchange, the 70-acre financial district in Kuala Lumpur. Many contractor teams worked side by side across civil, structural, architectural and MEP packages. When work did not match the drawing, it got a Non-Conformance Report.

An NCR is not a complaint. It is a record with a fixed shape: what was inspected, what was out of tolerance, why it happened, what was done about it, and who checked the fix. It stays open until every one of those boxes is filled and signed. The point is that a defect cannot quietly disappear.

The loop, side by side

When I started building AI agent workflows, I found I was drawing the same loop.

Raise an NCR for anything out of toleranceThe agent does the work
Find the root cause, not just the symptomA checker tests the output against the definition

AI fails quietly

A badly poured column is visible. A wrong answer from a language model usually is not. It arrives in fluent sentences, in the right format, at the right time. The failures that matter in business automation are rarely crashes; they are confident outputs that are slightly wrong: a lead marked as spam, a total copied from the wrong row, a reply that promises something you do not offer.

That is why I do not judge an AI workflow by how good its best output looks. I judge it by what happens to its worst output.

1. Define correct before you build

On site, the drawing is the definition. Without it, an inspection is an opinion. Before I build a workflow I write the equivalent: what a correct result contains, what it must never contain, and which cases are out of scope and go straight to a person. For lead intake that might be: every enquiry gets a category from a fixed list, a contact detail that was really in the message, and no price that is not on your price list.

2. The checker is not the agent

A trade does not sign off its own work. In a workflow, the step that checks the output is separate from the step that produced it. Some checks are plain code: is the category on the list, does the number match the source, is the email address real text from the message. Some use a second model with a narrow question and no stake in the first answer. Either way, the agent's output is treated as unverified until it passes.

3. Failures go to a person

On site, an open NCR has an owner. In a workflow, an output that fails its check is retried once with the reason attached, and if it fails again it is routed to a named person with everything they need to decide. It is never sent on silently, and it is never dropped.

4. Every run leaves a record

An NCR file is the reason a consultant can sign off a floor months later. A workflow keeps the same kind of record: the input, the output, which checks passed or failed, who handled the exceptions, and what each run cost. When something goes wrong, the question is not "what did the AI do?" but "which step let it through?", and the log answers it.

5. A cap, like a wind cutoff

On rope-access work at Rotol Group, an anemometer reading above the limit stops the job, whatever the schedule says. Every workflow I build has the same kind of hard stop: a budget per run and per day. If it is reached, the workflow pauses and tells a person, rather than spending its way through a loop.

What this means for a business

None of this makes a workflow clever. It makes it safe to rely on. The useful question for any business automating a process is not whether the AI can do the task, but what happens on the day it gets the task wrong. If the answer is "a person sees it, with the reason, before a client does", the workflow is ready.

That is the standard I bring from site to every AI automation project.
